Look at the pale cups in this series and you'll see a fine web of lines across a cream-white surface, like the craquelure on an old painting. It's the kind of thing most people would call crackle glaze. On these pieces, though, it isn't glaze at all.

**Is crackle glaze actually a glaze?** Usually, yes: a true crackle glaze is a glassy coat made to craze into a network of fine cracks as it cools. But on A Penny For Your Pottery's pale pieces, the crackled look isn't a coat of glass. It's worked into the clay-and-slip surface itself, through the maker's own process, and there's no glaze layer over it.

## How it's made, in the maker's words

The surface comes from a water-drip technique, not a glaze. As the maker describes it: water is dripped on in an incremental way over dried clay and slips on one side, letting the decoration form through that process, with various clays and materials. **It is not a glaze.**

That's the whole distinction. A crackle *glaze* would sit on the pot as a thin layer of glass, crazed into a web as it cools. Here the web is in the clay-and-slip layer itself: the decoration the drips left behind, rather than a coating that cracked.

## Telling it apart from a crack in the clay

Two different things can look similar on a base or a wall:

- The **crackled surface** is an even, web-like network across the piece, most visible where light rakes across it: the worked decoration.
- A **structural hairline crack** is an occasional single line in the body or base. These pages record those separately, and only as something seen in the photos, never as a flaw.
